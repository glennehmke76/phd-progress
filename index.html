Eastern Hooded Plover recovery

This is one paper with a long Supplement, plus a smaller Mornington Peninsula case study that began life as a Supplement section and has, as these things do, started asking for its own paper. The paper takes almost four decades of biennial Eastern Hooded Plover counts in Victoria (1984–2022) and applies them to two evidence questions: what the trajectories tell us about decline and recovery, and how scenario-based comparisons might inform conservation-impact and continuing-dependence questions. Observed recovery alone does not establish conservation impact. Data rescue, spatial reconstruction and monitoring design are central to the account, though a fuller treatment of practical monitoring methods could warrant a publication of its own.

The main paper and Supplement are structurally complete working drafts. The Mornington strand is still an early outline: most of its analyses have been run, but the writing is not well developed. Comments are best made on the Word copies from 26 October, which I have deliberately left unregenerated as a stable base. The main paper · where the analysis stands

After about five years of reconstruction and vetting, the data side is largely settled relative to where it began. There is still significant work on discrepancies and checks, but the principal data foundation is established. Survey routes have been reconstructed from heterogeneous spreadsheets into stable route and super-route units in a PostgreSQL/PostGIS database, vetted against map atlases, and the database structure and vetting protocol are now documented as Appendix S1-D. The trajectory model is a three-level hierarchical generalised additive mixed model. Since the first review copy, a complementary route-smooth model has been verified and promoted to a main result, so trajectories can be read at both regional and super-route scale. All 27 super-route trajectories now have journal-style plates.

Provisionally, all three regions declined through to the late 1990s and then diverged. Central Victoria recovered strongly, Western Victoria more modestly, and Eastern Victoria showed little clear recovery after the steepest decline. Naive summed counts and densities would have told a different story where survey coverage changed; once coverage stabilised after 2010 they track the model. The three-generation window results depend on which generation length is used, so those sensitivities are kept explicit. These are Victorian results relevant to assessment. They are not a Criterion A assessment of the species or the eastern subspecies.

The conservation-impact strand borrows Green Status ideas but is deliberately labelled as trajectory-based analogues of Legacy, Dependence and Gain, not formal Green Status metrics. Two rate methods are still kept open, and I want independent statistical review before I settle them.

Mornington Peninsula case study · where it stands

The short note uses an applied dataset to show pitfalls in analytical practice: how processing choices, the monitored frame and model specification change the trend you get from the same records. It highlights why those choices need explicit consideration when analysing unstructured data, particularly where coverage and recording effort increase over time. Frame composition and the observation process need to be considered alongside the fitted model; the biennial and opportunistic-data strands provide different demonstrations of that problem. Over the 2008–2022 window the Mornington biennial frame grew from core ocean-beach habitat into bay shores, and the Hooded Plover data then give very different answers depending on how they are summarised. Summed counts rise slightly (+0.8% a year), mean route density falls (−6.7%), and a pooled model with no route effect gives a statistically supported decline (−3.5%). Using all routes, the route-intercept model gives −1.3% a year and the fixed-composition habitat model −0.9%; a separate analysis restricted to core habitat gives −0.5%. Each interval spans zero, but the core-habitat estimate refers to a different monitored frame. I describe this as frame-composition confounding: the estimate follows the frame. It does not show the population was stable.

The comparison with Red-capped Plover is a diagnostic contrast, not a control. The two species use different parts of the Peninsula’s three ecological systems (ocean coast, Port Phillip Bay and Westernport), a classification agreed with local collaborators.

A reproduction of a published citizen-science trend analysis (Wijewardhana et al. 2022) sits alongside as supporting context. Its species-trend results reproduce from the archived data and code. The Red-capped Plover increase is positive in most variants, but whether its interval excludes zero depends on how the observation process is modelled. That is a statement about model sensitivity, not about error. The same method has also been applied to current Atlas of Living Australia extracts; those results are still to be reviewed, and I am reading them only as annual reported counts, not population trends.

Still to do: system-level analyses (the ladder within each system, and a decomposition of how much of a pooled trend is change within systems versus changing composition), graphic improvements and a context map. Where it belongs is open: a compact Supplement case study, a short methods paper, or set aside while the two priority papers are submitted.

Taxon composites

The taxon-composite methods are now functional and producing applied results, including the Striated Grasswren and Dusky Robin examples. They allow evidence from different survey streams to contribute to one taxon’s trajectory, with the member models, weighting alternatives, sensitivity and rationale visible together in the evidence dossiers.

A substantial part of the development has been working out what should determine each member’s influence. The F family uses fitted-model precision, with change-rate and whole-trajectory measures examined separately. The Q family addresses evidence fitness for the intended estimate, including temporal span and sampling distribution; a precise fit is not automatically adequate or representative evidence. Equal weighting remains a transparent reference.

The hierarchical evidence-fitness–precision approach combines those ideas: evidence fitness establishes the primary allocation, while fitted precision can refine it within declared limits without reversing the evidence-fitness ordering. These alternatives have working calculations, sensitivity tests and dossier presentations. They remain methods to assess in context, rather than a universally adopted default.

This replaces some implicit discretion with reproducible, data-driven alternatives that experts can compare through documented evidence assessment and elicitation. It does not remove the need for judgement: the target, admissible evidence, weighting assumptions and final selection still need to be justified and recorded. Habitat and spatial diagnostics are available as context, but a defensible habitat-weighting rule remains outstanding; those diagnostics are not silently converted into weights.

A selected taxon composite can then enter the ecological-group indicator alongside a trajectory from a single taxon model. It contributes once at the declared analytical grain, with its internal weighting and uncertainty retained, rather than each survey stream receiving a separate vote in the group index.

Multi-taxon ecological-group indices

Indicator selection and ecological membership

The membership protocol separates the ecological question—what group is being represented and which taxa belong—from the availability or direction of fitted trends. It brings together an explicit group definition, attributed literature and habitat-association evidence, structured review workbooks and recorded decisions. Where a review is intended to be outcome-blind, membership is assessed before exposing the downstream indicator results.

Ecological eligibility and analytical realisation are distinct. Much of the ecological evidence is at species level; the workflow then resolves the applicable ultrataxa and their reviewed trajectories, retaining exclusions, uncertainty and unavailable inputs explicitly. This keeps the intended ecological group distinct from the subset for which a defensible index can presently be calculated. The Eastern Mallee completed example also makes my own outcome exposure visible, rather than representing it as independent blind review.

Composite-index development

The group index combines compatible relative trajectories geometrically, with one contribution per realised analytical member. Single-model and selected taxon-composite inputs can be used together. The resulting statistic describes the average relative trajectory of that declared membership; it is not a change in total bird abundance.

The development now includes diagnostics and visualisations that separate estimation uncertainty in the group trajectory from between-taxon heterogeneity, membership limitations and sensitivity to shared estimation error and the reporting baseline. Leave-one-out analyses show how much particular members influence the result. Heterogeneity displays show how differently members behave even where the average looks simple. Those are different questions and should not be collapsed into one confidence band.

The evidence dossiers bring the index, uncertainty, member trajectories, heterogeneity and influence together for interpretation. The uncertainty procedures and dependence assumptions remain explicit and provisional where required; the working outputs do not establish that every source of uncertainty has been captured.

What the full-provenance view makes visible

The worked example starts with fitted taxon models and follows their review, optional composite alternatives, attributed selections, ecological-group membership, analytical inputs and the resulting multi-taxon indicator. Range construction, site construction and fitting the original models sit upstream of this package’s starting point.

The main development is the connection between the summary and the underlying evidence. A reader can move from the indicator back to the inputs and judgements that produced it, or inspect an earlier decision stage without having to reconstruct the whole workflow. It includes my own working judgements and how they were recorded and validated, as well as the reader-facing dossiers and supporting provenance.

This is a worked exemplar for discussion, not a request to approve its conclusions. The indicator result is provisional. Seeing the completed decisions and downstream results makes a reader outcome-exposed for this Eastern Mallee example; they would therefore not subsequently review those same decisions blind. That does not, by itself, prevent a separately designed outcome-blind membership exercise for a different region.

Data-science strategy and conservation evidence

This is a priority for further development later this week. The organisation-agnostic perspective has moved beyond the earlier outline to a new conceptual-review checkpoint, working under the title From data to defensible claims in applied conservation. There is not yet a manuscript or a circulated conceptual-review package.

The perspective · current conceptual framing

The central working proposition treats applied-conservation data science as a maintained capability for generating and appraising empirical evidence: connecting questions and observations through suitable inference to specified claims, testing their adequacy for intended uses, and keeping their basis recoverable. It is a proposed account of the function, not a claim that the discipline already has one agreed definition or that one team owns the whole process.

The current presentation uses five appraisal questions: what exactly is claimed; whether the inference is valid; whether any classification rules have been applied correctly; whether the result is adequate for its intended use; and whether its basis can be inspected and updated. A complementary pathway shows how questions, observations, representations and inference lead to a claim and its appraisal, before any decision or action.

Published trend-inference, extinction-risk and species-distribution examples are being considered to test the argument. The new checkpoint identifies conditional evidence needs rather than prescribing another broad literature search. The presentation, example choices and later readers still need author review. The initial public argument remains general and organisation-agnostic; the separate organisational premise and citation analyses are not being imported into it.
